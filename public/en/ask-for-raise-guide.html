How to Ask for a Raise (Step-by-Step Guide)

September 1, 2026

Asking for a raise is one of the most intimidating conversations in your career. But it's also one of the most important. If you never ask, you'll likely never get one—and you'll lose thousands of dollars over your lifetime.

In this step-by-step guide, I'll show you exactly how to ask for a raise with confidence, preparation, and proven strategies.

Why You Should Ask for a Raise

  • Employees who ask for raises earn significantly more over time
  • If you don't ask, your salary stays flat (or below inflation)
  • Asking shows you're confident and value your contributions
  • It sets a precedent for future negotiations

Step 1: Evaluate Your Timing

Timing is everything. The best times to ask for a raise are:

  • ✅ After a major accomplishment (e.g., big project, promotion)
  • ✅ During your annual performance review
  • ✅ After receiving positive feedback from a client or manager
  • ✅ When the company is doing well financially
  • ✅ At the start of a new fiscal year (when budgets are set)

Worst times to ask:

  • ❌ During a company layoff or budget cut
  • ❌ Right after you've made a mistake
  • ❌ When your manager is stressed or overwhelmed
  • ❌ Before you have any tangible achievements

Step 2: Do Your Research (Know Your Worth)

Before asking, research the market rate for your role and experience level. Use:

  • Glassdoor
  • LinkedIn Salary
  • Payscale
  • BLS (Bureau of Labor Statistics)
  • Industry reports

Example: If you're a Marketing Manager with 5 years of experience in NYC, the market rate might be $85,000-$95,000. If you're earning $80,000, you have a strong case for a raise.

Step 3: Quantify Your Achievements

Make a list of your accomplishments from the past year. Focus on:

  • Revenue generated or saved
  • Projects completed on time
  • Skills learned or certifications earned
  • Team leadership or mentoring
  • Client satisfaction or retention

Example: "I increased revenue by 30% and reduced costs by 15% through process improvements."

Step 4: Prepare Your Case (Write It Down)

Write a one-page summary of your achievements, market research, and the specific raise you're requesting.

Example template:

Request: I'm requesting a salary increase from $80,000 to $90,000.

Market Research: Market rate for my role is $85,000-$95,000.

Achievements: Increased revenue by 30%, led 3 successful projects, earned Google Analytics certification.

Value: I'm ready to take on more responsibility and deliver even bigger results.

Step 5: Practice Your Pitch (Out Loud)

Practice your conversation with a friend, family member, or in front of a mirror. You need to sound confident, not apologetic.

Step 6: Schedule a Meeting

Don't ambush your manager. Send a calendar invite and give them a heads up:

Email example:

"Hi [Manager's Name],

I'd like to schedule a 15-minute meeting to discuss my contributions to the team and my compensation. I've prepared some information I'd like to share. Would [date/time] work for you?"

Thanks,
[Your Name]

Step 7: Have the Conversation (Use a Script)

Here's a proven script you can adapt:

Opening: "Thank you for meeting with me. I'd like to discuss my compensation and the value I've brought to the team this year."

Evidence: "I've achieved [specific results], and I've also earned [certification/skill]. I believe my contributions have exceeded expectations."

Research: "I've researched the market rate for my role, and based on my experience, I believe a salary of $90,000 is fair."

Close: "I'm excited about continuing to contribute to the team, and I'm confident this adjustment reflects the value I bring."

Step 8: Handle Their Response (3 Scenarios)

Scenario 1: They Say Yes

Great! Thank them, confirm the amount and timeline, and ask for it in writing.

What to say: "Thank you! I appreciate your support. Could you send me a written confirmation of the new salary?"

Scenario 2: They Say No (But Offer Something Else)

Ask about alternative forms of compensation (bonus, vacation, flexibility, professional development).

What to say: "I understand the budget may be limited. Would you consider a one-time bonus or additional vacation days instead?"

Scenario 3: They Say No (With No Explanation)

Ask for specific feedback and a timeline for revisiting the conversation.

What to say: "I'd appreciate feedback on what I need to do to qualify for a raise in the future. Can we set a follow-up meeting in 3 months?"

Step 9: Follow Up in Writing

After the conversation, send a thank-you email summarizing what was discussed.

Email example:

"Hi [Manager's Name],

Thank you for the conversation today. I appreciate the time you took to discuss my contributions and compensation. As discussed, [summarize outcome]. I look forward to continuing to contribute to the team.

Best regards,
[Your Name]"

Step 10: What If They Don't Agree?

If you don't get the raise, don't burn bridges. Instead:

  • Ask for a clear plan to earn the raise
  • Set a follow-up date (3-6 months)
  • Continue to document your achievements
  • Start exploring other opportunities if needed

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • ❌ Asking without preparation
  • ❌ Using emotional arguments ("I need more money")
  • ❌ Comparing your salary to a coworker's
  • ❌ Threatening to quit unless you get the raise
  • ❌ Forgetting to quantify your achievements
  • ❌ Asking during a bad time (company layoffs)

Final Thoughts

Asking for a raise is a professional skill. With preparation, timing, and confidence, you can make a strong case for why you deserve it.

FAQ

1. When should I ask for a raise?

After a major accomplishment, during your annual review, or when the company is doing well financially.

2. How much should I ask for?

Research the market rate and ask for 10-20% above your current salary, depending on your experience.

3. Should I use a script?

Yes, but customize it to your situation. Practice out loud so it sounds natural.

4. What if they say no?

Ask for feedback, set a follow-up date, and continue to document your achievements.

5. Can I ask for a raise via email?

Better to ask in person, then follow up with an email. Email alone is less effective.
